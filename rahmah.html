Number 3. Children Need a Safe Place

Children need two things from their parents:

A safe place

“When I am scared or upset, I can come to you.”

Support to grow

“You believe in me and help me become independent.”

Psychology calls this

Secure attachment

A strong, safe bond between a child and a parent. Children with this bond find it easier to ask for help, try new things, and learn from mistakes.

Pause and think

If your child had something difficult or embarrassing to tell you, would they feel comfortable coming to you?

Step 2 · Stay calm

Number 5. Children Have Big Feelings

Children are still learning how to handle their feelings. They may:

  • Cry when they are frustrated
  • Yell when they are angry
  • Struggle to calm down
  • Act before they think

Psychology calls this

Co-regulation

We stay calm and help our child calm down, again and again, until they slowly learn to do it on their own.

Example

Your four-year-old cries because you said no to candy.

“I know you’re sad. You really wanted candy. We are not having candy right now.”

The rule did not change. Your child learned that their feelings matter.

Our job is to teach children how to handle their feelings, not to punish them for having feelings.

6

Step 2 · Stay calm

Number 6. Be Kind, But Keep Your Rules

Example

Your child cries when screen time is over.

✕ Instead of✓ Try

✕Instead of: “Stop crying! You’re being ridiculous!”

✓Try: “I know you’re upset. You wanted more time. But screen time is finished for today.”

This teaches two things:

  • Feelings are okay.
  • Rules still matter.

We can be gentle and firm at the same time.

7

Step 2 · Stay calm

Number 7. Children Learn by Watching Us

  • When we yell, children learn to yell.
  • When we stay calm, children learn how to stay calm.
  • When we apologize, children learn to apologize.

When you feel anger rising

  1. Make sure your child is safe.
  2. Say: “A‘udhu billahi min ash-shaytan ir-rajim.” Sahih al-Bukhari 6115
  3. If you are standing, sit down. Sunan Abi Dawud 4782
  4. Breathe slowly. Answer when you are calm.

Step 3 · Teach

Number 8. Five Questions to Ask Yourself

  1. Is my home safe, for my child’s body and feelings?
  2. Do I notice and praise good behavior?
  3. Are my rules clear and fair?
  4. Am I expecting what my child can manage at this age?
  5. Am I taking care of myself, too?

Based on research

These questions come from the five core principles of Triple P, the Positive Parenting Program. Psychologists use it to help parents with behavior problems while building a strong relationship with their children.

Source: CrimeSolutions, National Institute of Justice

9

Step 3 · Teach

Number 9. Discipline Means Teaching

The goal of discipline is to teach children how to make better choices.

Good discipline includes:

  • Clear rules
  • Calm reminders
  • Fair consequences
  • Praise for good choices
  • Teaching what to do next time

Example

Your child hits their brother.

“You cannot hit your brother. If you’re angry, you can tell him to stop, ask me for help, or take a break.”

Pause and think

Ask yourself: What is my child learning from my response?

Step 3 · Teach

Number 11. Teach Islam with Love, Not Fear Alone

We want our children to:

  • Love Allah
  • Feel comfortable asking questions
  • Understand why we practice Islam
  • Know right from wrong
  • Know that Allah is Ar-Rahman, the Most Merciful

Example

Your child lies about praying salah.

✕ Instead of✓ Try

✕Instead of: “You are a terrible Muslim!”

✓Try: “Prayer is important, and telling the truth is important too. I want to understand why you didn’t pray. Let’s talk about how we can do better tomorrow.”

We want our children to follow Islam because they love and understand it, not only because they fear our anger.

12

Step 3 · Teach

Number 12. Guilt and Shame Are Different

Guilt

“I did something bad.”

The child knows the action was wrong. Guilt helps a child make things right.

Shame

“I am bad.”

The child believes something is wrong with them. Shame makes a child want to hide.

A behavior can be wrong without the child being bad.

Research · 2025

A 2025 review of research with more than 19,000 children and teens found that harsh or difficult parent–child relationships go along with more shame. Warm relationships go along with healthy guilt.

Source: van Eickels and colleagues, Child Development, 2025

Step 3 · Teach

Number 13. Words Can Hurt More Than We Realize

Words that shame children:

  • “Why can’t you be like your brother?”
  • “You are so lazy.”
  • “You always embarrass me.”
  • “What’s wrong with you?”
  • “What will people say?”
  • “You should be ashamed of yourself.”

Pause and think

If our children are afraid to tell us about their small mistakes, how will they tell us about their big ones?

Step 3 · Teach

Number 14. Correct the Behavior, Not the Child

✕ Instead of✓ Try

✕Instead of: “You’re a bad child!”

✓Try: “That was a bad choice.”

✕Instead of: “You’re so lazy!”

✓Try: “Let’s work on getting this done.”

✕Instead of: “You’re a liar!”

✓Try: “You were not honest with me. We need to work on telling the truth.”

✕Instead of: “Stop embarrassing me!”

✓Try: “We need to talk about what happened.”

✕Instead of: “Why can’t you be like your sister?”

✓Try: “Let’s focus on what you can do better.”

✕Instead of: “Allah will hate you!”

✓Try: “Allah loves it when we say sorry and try again.”

Be firm about the rule. Be kind to the child.

Pause and think

Try it: think of one thing you say when you are frustrated. How could you say it in a firm but kind way?

15

Step 3 · Teach

Number 15. Every Child Grows at Their Own Pace

Children are different.

  • Young children need more help with feelings.
  • Older children need guidance and more independence.
  • Some children learn skills more slowly.
  • Children with ADHD, autism, or other challenges may need extra support.

Fair does not always mean the same. It means giving each child what they need.

Pause and think

Does my child know how to do what I’m asking, or do they need help learning it?

Step 4 · Make things right

Number 16. Parents Make Mistakes, Too

Sometimes parents:

  • Yell when they are stressed
  • Get angry over small mistakes
  • Say something hurtful
  • Blame a child unfairly
  • Take out their frustration on their children

Psychology calls this

Repair

Making things right after someone has been hurt. Strong relationships are not the ones with no mistakes. They are the ones where mistakes get repaired.

When we hurt our children, it is important to make things right.

17

Step 4 · Make things right

Number 17. Saying Sorry Does Not Make You Weak

When parents apologize, children learn:

  • Everyone makes mistakes.
  • It is important to admit when we are wrong.
  • We should care when we hurt someone.
  • We can work to make things better.
  • Respect goes both ways.

Research · 2025

In a study of 288 teenagers, a real apology from a parent, one focused on how the teen was hurt, led to more forgiveness and a better relationship. A defensive apology (“I’m sorry, but…”) did not work as well. There was little sign that apologizing made parents lose authority.

Source: Robichaud and colleagues, Journal of Research on Adolescence, 2025

A good parent does not have to be a perfect parent.

18

Step 4 · Make things right

Number 18. How to Apologize to Your Child

A real apology has four parts:

  1. 1

    Say what you did.

    “I yelled at you.”

  2. 2

    Take responsibility.

    “I should not have done that.”

  3. 3

    Say sorry.

    “I’m sorry I hurt your feelings.”

  4. 4

    Try to do better.

    “Next time, I will try to stay calm.”

Avoid saying

“I’m sorry, but you made me angry.”

The word “but” puts the blame back on your child.

Example

Your child spills milk by accident, and you yell.

“I’m sorry I yelled earlier. You spilled the milk by accident, and I got too angry. I was having a hard day, and that was not your fault. You did not deserve to be yelled at. Next time, I will try to stay calm.”

Give your child time. They may not feel better right away.

Number 19. A Moment to Reflect

Pause and think

Think back to when you were a child. Was there a time your mother or father was harsh with you when you did not deserve it? What would it have meant to you if they came back later and said: “I am sorry. I was wrong. You did not deserve that”?

You do not need to answer out loud.

We may not be able to change how we were raised. But we can choose how we raise our children.
